\section{Introduction}

The diffusion of generative AI capabilities since 2022 has reignited a
long-standing question in political sociology: do technological shocks
to occupational structure erode trust in democratic institutions?
Within-country evidence on automation exposure points consistently to
political consequences. \citet{ColantoneStanig2018} and
\citet{AnelliColantoneStanig2021} show that local-labour-market
exposure to industrial robots predicts populist voting and reduced
political trust. \citet{FreyBergerChen2018} document analogous patterns
for U.S. county-level robot installations. The mechanism in these
studies is structural-grievance: technology generates losers, and
losers re-route their political behaviour through grievance channels
\citep{GidronHall2017,Kurer2020}.

What does this look like for generative AI? Two features of the
literature so far suggest the question is genuinely open. First,
existing studies use \emph{robot} exposure measures
\citep{Felten2021,Webb2020,AcemogluRestrepo2020} that are
concentrated in manufacturing tasks, which is the
opposite of GenAI's white-collar/services bias. Second, almost all the
empirical work is single-country, leaving open whether the
cross-national pattern is the same as the within-country one. The
distinction matters: as \citet{BellJones2015} and especially
\citet{SchmidtCatranFairbrother2016} argue, when between- and
within-country effects of a covariate diverge, conventional
multilevel models that constrain them to be equal answer a different
substantive question than the one researchers usually pose.

This paper makes the within--between distinction central. I pool six
rounds of the European Social Survey (R6 through R11, 2012--2024)
and merge them at the individual level with the ILO--NASK 2025
GenAI exposure scores at 4-digit ISCO-08 \citep{Gmyrek2025}. I
aggregate per-individual exposure to a country-year mean
$\bar E_{ct}$, decompose it into a between component $\bar E_c$ and
a within component $E_{ct}-\bar E_c$, and estimate three-level
multilevel models with random intercepts at country and country-year.
The headline result is that the between- and within-coefficients
are sharply different: the cross-sectional positive correlation
between AI exposure and trust does not carry through to within-country
exposure shifts. The Mundlak Wald test rejects equality, and an
education-moderation test of the within-effect returns a precise null
that falsifies both the substitution and curator readings.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:theory} draws on
performance theories of trust, structural-grievance accounts, and the
robot-exposure empirical tradition to derive the hypotheses.
Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the ESS panel, the ILO--NASK exposure
scores with their 2023/2025 vintage rule, and the macro and
institutional covariates. Section~\ref{sec:methods} sets out the
three-level model, the within--between specification, and the
inference apparatus. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the M0--M6
progression, the Mundlak Wald, the cross-level interaction, and the
robustness battery. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the
finding, discusses the endogeneity concerns the design cannot
foreclose, and notes limitations.
