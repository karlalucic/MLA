\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Variance decomposition}\label{sec:results-vc}

The empty model M0 places trust variance roughly one-quarter at the
country level and one-fortieth at the country-year level. Fit on the M3a
common sample ($N=165{,}969$, 30 countries), M0 gives
$\rho_3=0.243$, $\rho_2=0.025$, and an L1 share of $0.732$. The L3 share sits inside
the $0.15$--$0.25$ band that \citet{SchmidtCatranFairbrother2016} report
for European trust outcomes; the L2 share, while modest, is above the
soft floor below which a within--between decomposition adds little to a
country-fixed-effects specification.

Adding individual controls (M1) reduces L1 residual variance by 5\% and
L2 country-year variance by 17\%, while the L3 ICC barely moves. Adding
the macro covariates and round dummies (M2) raises the cumulative L2
reduction to 61\% and L3 to 13\%; the round dummies absorb the global
upward trend in trust between R6 and R11, leaving the L2 VPC at
$0.011$. The contrast between M3a (with round dummies) and M3b (without)
in Section~\ref{sec:results-headline} surfaces what this absorption
costs interpretively. Figure~\ref{fig:variance} visualises the
decomposition across M0$\to$M6.

\subsection{The within--between headline}\label{sec:results-headline}

The Mundlak decomposition (M3a) is the substantive heart of the
analysis: between- and within-country slopes diverge sharply.
Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the full M0$\to$M6 progression. The M3a
point estimates are
\[
  \hat\gamma_W = +0.023 \;(\mathrm{SE}\;1.123),
  \qquad
  \hat\gamma_B = +11.239 \;(\mathrm{SE}\;3.427),
\]
with a Wald test of equality $\chi^2(1)=9.66$, $p=0.002$. The
within-country slope is statistically indistinguishable from zero,
leaving H2's predicted within erosion unsupported; the between-country
slope is large, positive, and significant.

Because both Mundlak terms enter on the native ILO--NASK $0$--$1$ scale
rather than the individual exposure's $z$-scale, the raw coefficients
need translating against the dispersion in Table~\ref{tab:descriptives}.
The between-country SD of $\bar E_c$ is $\approx 0.02$ and its observed
range is $0.27$--$0.34$, so $\hat\gamma_B=+11.24$ means that a country
one SD higher in mean exposure has about $+0.22$ points higher trust on
the composite---roughly a quarter of the composite's $0.85$ SD---and
that the most- versus least-exposed country differ by $\approx 0.79$
composite points, close to a full composite SD. The within margin moves
on a far narrower base: $E_{ct}-\bar E_c$ has SD $\approx 0.01$ and spans
only $\approx 0.04$ units across 2012--2024. Expressed per within-SD,
$\hat\gamma_W$ is $+0.0002$ with a $95\%$ interval of $\pm 0.022$
composite points ($\approx\pm 0.03$ composite SD), and the design is
powered ($80\%$, $\alpha=0.05$) to detect a within slope of about
$0.03$ composite points. The within null is therefore precise on the
standardised scale, not an artefact of imprecision; what it cannot
surmount is the small range of within variation that survives once the
common European time trend is absorbed (the M3a$\to$M3b contrast below
makes this concrete).

The sign of $\hat\gamma_B$ is the opposite of H1, which points to
selection rather than experience. Countries with higher mean GenAI
exposure---typically wealthier, more service-oriented, and
institutionally stronger---report \emph{higher} trust, not lower. The
cross-sectional correlation is bound up with what makes a country
high-exposure in the first place. Removing round dummies (M3b) inflates
the within slope to $\hat\gamma_W = +1.46$, attributing the global
2012--2024 trend in trust to within-country exposure changes; the gap
between M3a and M3b therefore clarifies that within-country exposure
shifts identifiable \emph{separately} from the time trend are not
detectably correlated with trust.

Figure~\ref{fig:caterpillar} shows the country random intercepts
(BLUPs) from M3a. Cross-country heterogeneity persists after the L1,
L2, and Mundlak controls: Northern European countries cluster in the
positive tail (Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland, Iceland, Switzerland,
the Netherlands), Eastern and Southern European countries in the
negative tail. The M3a sample includes 30 of the 36 ESS countries; six
drop via macro listwise deletion (Israel, Russia, Albania, Ukraine,
Kosovo, the United Kingdom; see Section~\ref{sec:data}).

The individual-level coefficient also runs against H3.
$\hat\beta_{\mathrm{genai}}=+0.015$ ($\mathrm{SE}\;0.002$) is small,
positive, and significant across M1--M3b; H3 predicted a negative sign.
The composition channel through which AI-exposed individuals would
themselves report lower trust does not run as the single-country
literature would predict, and the small individual-level positive sign
is at least consistent in direction with the large between-country
slope.

\subsection{Cross-level interaction (H4) and institutional moderation (H5)}

M5 adds the within-exposure $\times$ ISCED education interaction and
returns a precise null: coefficient $-0.142$ ($\mathrm{SE}\;0.130$,
$p=0.275$). Neither H4a (substitution) nor H4b (curator) is supported;
the data prefer H4$_0$. Figure~\ref{fig:interaction} plots the
predicted shift in trust at each ISCED level over a plausible range of
within-country exposure deviations. The conditional slopes fan out
slightly across ISCED levels---higher-educated respondents react
marginally less positively---but the spread is not statistically
distinguishable from parallel, consistent with the retained null. (They pass through the origin by
construction: the figure shows only the conditional shift attributable
to the within-deviation, not total predicted trust.)

\begin{figure}[!h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/fig3_conditional_within_x_education.pdf}
  \caption{Conditional within-country exposure effect on trust by
  ISCED level (M5). The point estimates suggest higher-educated
  respondents react less positively to within-country exposure
  increases, but the interaction coefficient is not statistically
  significant ($p=0.275$) and the 95\% confidence interval on the
  difference spans zero, so H4$_0$ cannot be rejected.}
  \label{fig:interaction}
\end{figure}

For H5, the OECD EPL\,v1 (2007) baseline covers 23 ESS countries, of
which 22 fit M6 (the United Kingdom drops via the same Eurostat
unemployment gap as in M3a). The within $\times$ EPL interaction is
positive ($+1.64$) but imprecise ($\mathrm{SE}\;1.44$, $p\approx 0.26$):
with 22 L3 units, identification of an L3 moderator is intrinsically
limited, so H5 is neither supported nor rejected. On this OECD
subsample the within $\times$ ISCED interaction also flips from the M5
null to a small but nominally significant negative ($-0.318$,
$\mathrm{SE}\;0.141$, $p\approx 0.024$). The H4 conclusion rests on M5
because M5 uses the full European panel, while M6 conditions on
non-missing 2007 EPL---a sample that correlates with regime type and
labour-market structure, so the M6 estimate likely reflects
within-OECD selection.

\subsection{Mundlak Wald and robustness}\label{sec:results-robust}

The within $\neq$ between conclusion holds in every specification that
includes both exposure terms. Table~\ref{tab:mundlak} tabulates the
Mundlak Wald across M3a, M3b, M4, and M5; all have $p\leq 0.008$. The
largest test statistic is in M4 ($\chi^2(1)=18.42$), where the random
slope on individual exposure tightens the country-year fit.

\input{tables/tab3a_mundlak}

The headline pattern survives a battery of robustness checks
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). Each trust item separately replicates the
gap, with $\hat\gamma_B \in [11.0, 12.1]$ across \texttt{trstprl},
\texttt{trstlgl}, and \texttt{stfdem} and Mundlak Wald $p \leq 0.014$.
Across 30 country-leverage drops, $\hat\gamma_B \in [9.46, 12.24]$ and
Wald $p \leq 0.012$, so no single country drives the result. Dropping
the COVID-disrupted R10 round leaves $\hat\gamma_B = +10.52$ ($p=0.001$).
The vintage-static comparator (2025 scores throughout) gives
$\hat\gamma_B = +13.43$ ($p<0.001$), which is in fact \emph{larger} than
the headline---ruling out the R10$\to$R11 vintage shift as the source
of the cross-country pattern. Leave-one-out aggregation, which removes
the mechanical correlation between own and cell exposure, gives
$\hat\gamma_B = +10.43$ ($p=0.004$) and $\hat\gamma_W = +0.06$, so the
gap survives with only modest attenuation. Finally, a within-only
fixed-effects benchmark (\texttt{linearmodels.PanelOLS} with country
fixed effects and cluster-robust SEs) yields a coefficient on
$\bar E_{ct}$ of $-0.19$---opposite in sign to the Mundlak within slope
but, like it, negligible relative to the large between-country
coefficient, the same substantive conclusion.
