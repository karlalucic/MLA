\section{Theory and hypotheses}\label{sec:theory}

Three literatures converge on the question of whether AI exposure
affects institutional trust.

\paragraph{Performance theories of trust.}
\citet{Hetherington1998} and \citet{vanderMeer2017} argue that
trust in political institutions responds to perceived institutional
performance, including macroeconomic and structural conditions.
A technological shock that reduces the perceived ability of
institutions to deliver economic security---through job destruction
or through visible obsolescence of policy instruments---should
therefore reduce trust.

\paragraph{Structural-economic-grievance accounts.}
\citet{GidronHall2017} and \citet{Kurer2020} extend the performance
account by specifying the mechanism: technological change produces
labour-market losers, and losers re-route their political behaviour
through grievance. In the trust outcome the implication is that
individuals in occupations more exposed to substituting technologies
should report lower institutional trust net of compositional controls.

\paragraph{Robot exposure and political behaviour.}
The empirical literature on automation and political outcomes is
dominated by single-country studies of \emph{robot} exposure.
\citet{ColantoneStanig2018,AnelliColantoneStanig2021} document that
local-labour-market robot exposure predicts populist voting in
Western Europe; \citet{FreyBergerChen2018} report similar patterns for
the United States. The mechanism in this literature is broadly
consistent with structural grievance.

\paragraph{Contribution.}
The paper extends this tradition along three margins. (i) It substitutes
the manufacturing-biased robot exposure measures for the
white-collar/services-biased ILO--NASK GenAI exposure score
\citep{Gmyrek2025}, which is ISCO-08 4-digit native and admits a
2023$\to$2025 vintage shift that injects capability-driven
within-country time variation in $E_o$. (ii) It pools cross-national
data and applies the within--between specification of
\citet{BellJones2015} and \citet{SchmidtCatranFairbrother2016} so the
between-country level effect $\gamma_B$ and the within-country effect
$\gamma_W$ are estimated separately rather than constrained to be equal.
(iii) It specifies ex ante an underspecified-direction education-moderation
test that distinguishes a substitution reading (more-educated
respondents react more strongly to within-country exposure shifts) from
a curator reading (less strongly) from a precise null (no moderation).

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
\begin{description}
\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
  \item[H1 (between).] Across countries, higher mean AI exposure
    $\bar E_c$ is associated with lower mean institutional trust:
    $\gamma_B < 0$.
  \item[H2 (within).] Within countries, increases in AI exposure
    $(E_{ct}-\bar E_c)$ erode institutional trust: $\gamma_W < 0$.
  \item[H2a (decomposition).] $\gamma_W \neq \gamma_B$. Tested via
    a Mundlak Wald.
  \item[H3 (compositional).] Individuals in AI-exposed occupations
    report lower trust net of country context:
    $\beta_{\mathrm{genai}} < 0$.
  \item[H4.] The within-country exposure effect is moderated by
    individual education. Direction is theoretically
    underspecified; the paper evaluates three competing
    outcomes:
    \begin{description}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
      \item[H4a (substitution).] Higher-educated respondents react
        more strongly: negative interaction.
      \item[H4b (curator).] Higher-educated respondents react less
        strongly: positive interaction.
      \item[H4$_0$ (null).] No moderation; an apparent education
        gradient is compositional. A precise null falsifies both
        substitution and curator framings.
    \end{description}
  \item[H5 (institutional moderation).] The within-effect is
    attenuated where employment-protection legislation is stronger:
    positive coefficient on
    $\mathrm{EPL}_c \times (E_{ct}-\bar E_c)$.
\end{description}
